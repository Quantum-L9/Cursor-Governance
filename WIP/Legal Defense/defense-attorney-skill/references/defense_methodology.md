# Defense Methodology

## Objective
Find the strongest defense position actually supported by the record, then determine what investigation, legal research, evidentiary challenge, or courtroom strategy most increases the probability that the prosecution cannot meet its burden.

## 1. Record reconstruction
Create a neutral chronology before choosing a theory.

Capture:
- event;
- time or time range;
- actor;
- source;
- corroboration;
- contradiction;
- confidence;
- disputed/undisputed status.

When two sources differ, preserve both versions. Do not merge them into a synthetic fact.

## 2. Burden decomposition
For each count:
1. identify the jurisdiction and charging language;
2. identify every element;
3. identify the prosecution evidence for each element;
4. rate the evidentiary support as strong, contested, weak, absent, or UNKNOWN;
5. identify legal or factual defenses affecting that element.

Do not assume generic offense elements when the jurisdiction controls.

## 3. Evidence-to-element attack
For each material prosecution item, ask:
- What proposition is this offered to prove?
- Is that proposition necessary to an element?
- Is the item authentic and attributable?
- Is the source competent and reliable?
- Is there corroboration?
- Is there a competing innocent explanation?
- Does another record item contradict it?
- Is there missing evidence that should exist if the prosecution theory were true?

## 4. Theory generation
Generate only materially distinct theories. Examples of theory classes include:
- identity failure;
- intent failure;
- act failure;
- causation failure;
- possession/control failure;
- credibility failure;
- investigation contamination or incompleteness;
- lawful/innocent alternative explanation;
- affirmative defense when supported by governing law and facts.

A theory is not viable merely because it is possible. Rank by evidentiary support, legal viability, simplicity, consistency, and resilience under prosecution attack.

## 5. Adversarial test
For the leading theory:
- state the strongest prosecution rebuttal;
- identify the prosecution fact most damaging to the defense;
- identify any defense fact that requires an unsupported assumption;
- determine whether the theory survives without exaggeration.

If it fails, revise or discard it.

## 6. Leverage ranking
Prefer pressure points that affect:
1. an entire count or essential element;
2. a key witness or central exhibit;
3. multiple downstream facts;
4. admissibility of critical evidence;
5. the coherence of the prosecution narrative.

Do not confuse number of issues with strategic value.

## 7. Next-action rule
End substantial analysis with the single next action most likely to change the case posture: obtain a record, verify a timeline fact, research controlling authority, inspect metadata, retain an expert, prepare a motion issue, or develop a witness examination objective.
