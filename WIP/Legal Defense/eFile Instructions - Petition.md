### Why file now

N.C. Gen. Stat. 90-95(g) says the State can use a controlled-substance lab report without the analyst only if it follows the notice procedure and the defendant fails to file a written objection **with the court, with a copy to the State, at least five business days before the proceeding**. A timely objection prevents the statutory waiver and sends admissibility back to the applicable evidence rules. ([North Carolina General Assembly][1])

Assuming the probable-cause hearing remains **Friday, August 21**, Friday August 14 is the fifth preceding business day. **Filing August 13 gives us margin.**

* preservation of confrontation, hearsay, foundation, authentication, chain, methodology, reliability, scope, and expert objections;

### Important limit

This filing is deliberately **not** written to claim that the lab report automatically becomes inadmissible at the August 21 probable-cause hearing. North Carolina's specific probable-cause statute, G.S. 15A-611(b)(1), separately says scientific/expert reports concerning examinations or tests are admissible at that hearing. ([North Carolina General Assembly][5])

That is why paragraph 6 preserves the objection without handing the prosecutor an easy argument that our filing misstates the PC-hearing statute. The bigger win here is **preventing waiver and preserving the analyst issue for every stage where it actually bites.**

### Devil's Advocate

The State's best response is: *"15A-611 expressly permits the lab report at probable cause."* They're probably right about that narrow hearing issue. Our answer is not to fight the statute uphill. **File the objection now, preserve the analyst/remote-testimony rights, then attack what the report actually proves and what it does not prove at the hearing.** That's the sharper blade.
